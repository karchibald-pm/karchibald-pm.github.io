The Trust Layer: Governed Data for Enterprise AI

As product manager, I led the requirements and helped scope and deliver a governed, self-service document-extraction tool that gives business areas direct access to the documents they need, with approvals, access control and data lineage built in, laying a trusted foundation for AI-powered search.

Live

Company
Global pharma company
My role
Sr. Manager, Business Analyst / Product Manager, overseeing 2 business analysts
Built by
Internal engineering team, with a design team in another time zone
Timeline
December 2024 discovery to April 2025 go-live
Users
Business areas that need to prepare and update documents
Product
AI data pipeline: governed document extraction for AI-powered search and retrieval

The problem

In a global pharma company, regulated documents must be drafted, updated and maintained with care. But business areas couldn't access the related documents directly. Every request went through a gatekeeper, who pulled the documents based on the user's permissions, and that go-between made the process slow: a single request could take three months to fulfill.

The business also wanted AI-powered search and retrieval, which would only be as trustworthy as the documents feeding it.

We set out to build a governed, self-service way in, so business areas could get the documents they needed without a go-between.

My approach

Leadership wanted it fast, so my job was to scope tightly, define requirements clearly and keep decisions moving.

  1. Scoped the MVP to one source system. The first release connected only to the core document system. Every other source went onto a Now / Next / Later roadmap, which kept the first release small enough to ship.
  2. Ran requirements workshops. Three weeks of workshops with the business system leads and stakeholders we were building for turned their needs into user stories before the first sprint began.
  3. Set a standard for every story. Each story had to state its why, its source, Given/When/Then acceptance criteria and non-functional requirements. People rarely cut corners on purpose; they skip what no one has defined.
  4. Designed governance into the workflow. The hardest question was who approves access to sensitive documents. As a team, we decided on a designated approver and backup for each area, with an automated authorization workflow and compliance checks, so users can reach only what they're allowed to and understand acceptable use.
  5. Led a lean team. My two BAs ran daily stand-ups and backlog refinement, and one adjusted their hours to overlap with the design team. A strong requirements baseline kept the work moving with the engineering team, even through team changes.

How it works

What shipped

  • Users create, monitor and manage their own extraction requests, select the documents they need and see when new versions arrive.
  • An approval workflow with compliance checks, so access to sensitive documents is approved by the right person.
  • End-to-end traceability and data lineage, with a live connection back to the source, so AI-powered search always knows where its content came from.

The delivery ran three weeks of requirements workshops, four sprints from February to April 2025, then user acceptance testing and go-live in April 2025.

Results

  • Delivered: went live in April 2025, about four months after discovery began.
  • Replaced: a gatekeeper process where a single request could take three months with self-service requests and an automated approval workflow, so business areas get the documents they need directly.
  • Built to scale: a reusable foundation for the other source systems on the roadmap.
  • Feedback: after I moved to my next role, the team reported that it works well.

What I learned

Enterprise AI is only as trustworthy as the data pipeline under it. Access, approvals and lineage are the unglamorous half of every AI product, and they decide whether AI can scale. And on a lean team, a tight scope and a solid requirements baseline keep the work moving, even when the team changes.
