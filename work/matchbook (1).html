MatchBook: An Explainable AI Marketplace

As AI product manager and Product & Design workstream lead, I owned the requirements, go-to-market strategy and design workstream for an AI matchmaker connecting retiring business owners with buyers, taken to Demo Day in an 11-week program.

Demo Day, October 2026

Program
PM Accelerator, 17-person cross-functional team
My role
AI Product Manager leading the Product & Design workstream, alongside two other AI PM–led workstreams and the MatchBook founder; led 2 designers
Timeline
11-week program, August to October 2026 (our team formed in week 3); Demo Day October 6, 2026
Users
Retiring small-business owners (sellers) and acquisition-minded buyers
Product
Two-sided marketplace with explainable AI matching

The problem

About 12 million U.S. businesses are owned by baby boomers nearing retirement, and more than 70% of owners have no succession plan (industry estimates). Sellers can't find buyers, buyers face stale listings they can't verify, and the best businesses never get listed because owners fear exposure. As our team put it: the silver tsunami has no matchmaker.

What customers told us

Our Voice of the Customer research with 16 buyers and sellers (my designers ran the surveys; I analyzed the results and delivered the readout) shaped the product:

  • Every buyer surveyed said they need verified financials to trust a match.
  • Sellers rated their odds of finding a buyer within a year at 2.8 out of 5.
  • 83% of sellers said a clear explanation of how matching works would build their trust.

We called it directional, not definitive: 16 people, not 1,600.

My approach

  1. Created the core product artifacts. The user journeys for buyers and sellers, the persona slides, a PRD of 141 traceable requirements, each with a user story and acceptance criteria (114 for the first MVP, 27 for Phase 2), the business model, and the project team's walkthrough deck.
  2. Led the design team. As a team, we aligned on the direction and scope. I made sure the designers had access to the rough prototype and worked with them to inventory the high-fidelity screens needed for the MVP. I set up design reviews and kept the work visible through weekly status. I also protected the designers from distractions and unnecessary changes that would have put the deadline at risk. Together they produced more than 50 high-fidelity screens in two weeks.
  3. Connected the workstreams. I set up a shared tracker so every workstream could see our progress, capacity and plan, and my team could work directly with our founder on design decisions. Roadblocks surfaced early and were cleared fast.
  4. Kept the global team aligned through the re-scope. After I raised the capacity risk in Week 7, I set up the conversations where the team decided to re-scope to a single buyer-led demo journey. Then I created the re-scoping document and walked everyone through the changes at our All Hands, so the whole team stayed focused on the MVP for Demo Day.
  5. Built the go-to-market strategy. Supply first, because sellers are scarce: concierge onboarding and accountant and advisor referrals for sellers, self-serve for buyers, confidentiality-led messaging, a landing page, a beta waitlist and a LinkedIn posting plan. I laid out every part of it in a go-to-market strategy deck.

What the team built for trust: a rule-based match score, 0 to 100 across nine categories, where AI explains every match but never does the math; seller identity hidden until the seller approves an introduction; financials behind an NDA; and AI-drafted introduction messages that are always labeled. The PRD I wrote included guardrails the team could trace back to as we built the MVP in a rapid development cycle.

Results

  • Led the design team that produced more than 50 high-fidelity screens in two weeks.
  • Created the PRD: 141 traceable requirements, each with a user story and acceptance criteria: 114 for the first MVP and 27 for Phase 2.
  • Developed the user journeys, business model and go-to-market strategy, including a LinkedIn and social media posting cadence.
  • Turned research into requirements: my designers ran the Voice of the Customer surveys with 16 buyers and sellers; I analyzed the results and delivered the readout, which shaped the product's trust features.
  • Demo Day: I contributed to the team's presentation on October 6, 2026, and it was a success.
  • Next step: the team plans to open the working MVP for testing to the program's community of 100,000+ members, along with the 20+ business owners on our beta waitlist.

What I learned

  • Raise capacity risks early, with data and a proposed fix. Our two designers delivered 50+ screens in two weeks, but the full scope still outpaced the timeline. Flagging it early gave the team room to re-scope instead of missing the deadline.
  • Be transparent about what an MVP can and can’t do. Clear labels and an honest roadmap keep users’ trust while the full product is still being built.
  • When time runs short, protect the core value proposition. For MatchBook, that was trust.
