Designing an AI Digital Assistant for Clinical Trials

I led the requirements and partnered on the user journey for a lifelike AI digital assistant that helps patients find and understand clinical trials, taking it from rough specs to a 100+ requirement baseline and an approved MVP design.

Designed to MVP; paused before launch

Company
Global pharma company, oncology
My role
Sr. Manager, Business Analyst, acting as Product Owner / Product Manager under the Director of Product; led 2 business analysts
Partners
A digital-human technology partner and a consulting partner
Timeline
2024
Users
Breast cancer patients and caregivers (originally healthcare professionals)
Product
Conversational AI with a lifelike digital person, on a patient-facing clinical trials website

The problem

The program’s goal was to increase enrollment in the company’s clinical trials, starting with breast cancer trials as the MVP. Trials need enough participants to run successfully, and successful trials are what bring new treatments to approval around the world. But patients and doctors struggle to find and understand clinical trials, which slows recruitment. The program set out to change that with a lifelike AI assistant that could talk people through their options with precision and compassion.

My approach

When I joined, the assistant was aimed at healthcare professionals, with only rough requirements and a launch planned in a few months. My role was to turn that into requirements the team could build from and trust.

  1. Laid out what a new kind of product needed. A lifelike digital assistant was new technology, so it needed a different lens than a typical system. I flagged the risk of moving forward on rough requirements and unpacked the key categories that needed to be documented: the assistant’s experience and the user’s, legal, AI behavior and non-functional requirements. That became a baseline the team could talk through and vet. The UX team mapped the end-to-end user journey, with me as a co-partner responsible for validating each step and identifying the gaps.
  2. Escalated when the pivot changed the product. Research from our CX team showed HCPs wouldn’t use the assistant, and the real need was with patients. That made it a new product, not a reskin, with a stricter legal lens. I escalated to leadership that the original scope and deadline were no longer sustainable, and they added three months. We redid the user journeys from the patient and caregiver perspective, creating several happy and unhappy paths, with empathy and plain language.
  3. Shaped the conversation design. After the pivot, I worked with our consulting partner on the voice flow, the if/then actions that guide the assistant’s conversations with potential patients, and took part in design reviews with our UX team to make sure the experience matched the requirements.
  4. Kept the work moving. When a requirements deliverable stalled, I escalated to the program leads and drafted the original requirements framework myself, so the team didn’t lose time.
  5. Worked through responsible-AI risks early. Because this was a new kind of patient-facing AI, we addressed the risks from the start. As a team, we identified the areas that would involve patient data, talked through how patient conversations would be stored, and worked with Legal on the boundaries for medical guidance.
  6. Protected the work with a baseline. With legal review running in parallel to the sprints, we baselined more than 100 requirements, so the work was protected and ready to resume at any point.

How it works

What the MVP covered

  • Answers to common questions about breast cancer clinical trials, on mobile and desktop
  • Links to trial search and pre-screening
  • The nurse navigator phone number for a human conversation
  • Feedback captured when a patient leaves, plus baseline metrics: pre-screen completion, where people drop off, and satisfaction

Later phases were planned on a Now / Next / Later roadmap: personalization, secure capture of personal information, more cancer types, trial matching, live-agent handoff, accessibility, more languages, and recognizing adverse-event language to route people to the safety reporting portal.

What happened

  • Designed to MVP: more than 100 requirements baselined, the patient journey and voice flow mapped, and the project brought to the demo-approval stage.
  • Paused before launch: the project was paused at the demo-approval stage and did not launch. Because the requirements were baselined, the work was ready to resume.

What I learned

  • System requirements tell you what a product must do; the user journey tells you what it must get right.
  • When your customer changes, your requirements change with it. The pivot to patients was a new product, and treating it that way earned the time to do it well.
  • For patient-facing AI, bring legal in early. Sequencing legal review before the build saves rework later. When that isn’t possible, a strong requirements baseline protects the work.
  • Voicing a risk isn't enough. Put it in the decision log with the cost of being wrong and an owner.
